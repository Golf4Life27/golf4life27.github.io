The 70% Rule and MAO: How to Calculate a Max Offer on a Fix and Flip

By KeyLedger Templates · Published 2026-10-08 · Updated 2026-10-30

Most house flips are won or lost on the day you make the offer. Pay too much and no amount of good rehab work saves the deal. That's why flippers and wholesalers use a maximum allowable offer (MAO): a ceiling you calculate before emotions kick in.

Illustrative numbers, not investment advice. Costs vary a lot by market and lender.

The 70% rule

MAO = ARV × 70% − repair costs

ARV (after-repair value) is what the house should sell for once it's fixed up, based on recent sold comparables. The other 30% is meant to cover your buying and selling costs, financing, holding costs and profit.

Example: ARV $295,000, repairs $48,000.

$295,000 × 0.70 = $206,500 − $48,000 = $158,500 MAO.

If you're wholesaling, subtract your assignment fee too: with a $10,000 fee, your MAO to the seller is $148,500. Many wholesalers open about 8% below that (roughly $136,600) to leave room to negotiate.

Step 1: Get the ARV right

The rule is only as good as the ARV. A solid ARV comes from sold comps (not listings) that are:

  • recent (ideally the last 3 to 6 months),
  • nearby (same neighborhood or subdivision),
  • similar (beds, baths, square footage, age, condition after rehab).

Convert each comp to price per square foot, adjust for obvious differences (an extra bathroom, a garage), then take the average or the median and multiply by the subject's square footage. Using the median protects you from one unusually high sale.

Step 2: Estimate repairs line by line

"About $50k" isn't a repair estimate. Walk the property and price each line: roof, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, kitchen, baths, flooring, paint, windows, landscaping, permits. Then add a 10–15% contingency. Surprises in old houses almost always cost money, never save it.

Step 3: Check the deal with full costs

The 70% rule is a shortcut. Before you commit, run a full cost budget. Here's the same house bought at $172,000 (above the rule's $158,500):

Cost Amount
Purchase price $172,000
Rehab $48,000
Buying closing costs (2%) $3,440
Financing (6 months interest at 11% on $160,000, plus 2 points) $12,000
Holding costs ($650 × 6 months) $3,900
Selling costs (5% agent, 1.5% closing, $2,500 staging) $21,675
Total cost $261,015

Profit = $295,000 − $261,015 = $33,985, about 11.5% of ARV.

So this deal works on paper even though it breaks the 70% rule, because financing and selling costs here are lower than the 30% the rule assumes. In a market with higher commissions or a longer rehab, it could be the other way around. That's the point: the rule is a filter; the full budget is the decision.

Step 4: Work backwards from your target profit

Instead of asking "what's my profit at this price?", ask "what's the most I can pay and still make $30,000?" With the cost structure above, that's roughly $175,900. Offering $172,000 leaves a small cushion. Offering $185,000 does not.

Step 5: Stress-test it

Flips rarely go exactly to plan. What happens to that $33,985 profit if things slip?

ARV change Rehab +0% Rehab +10% Rehab +20% Rehab +30%
−10% $6,403 $1,602 −$3,198 −$7,998
−5% $20,194 $15,394 $10,594 $5,794
0% $33,985 $29,185 $24,385 $19,585
+5% $47,776 $42,976 $38,176 $33,376

A 10% softer sale price wipes out most of the profit, and pairing it with a 20% rehab overrun turns the deal negative. If the downside row scares you, lower your offer or walk away.

Key takeaways

  • MAO = ARV × 70% − repairs (− your fee if wholesaling) is a fast first filter.
  • ARV from real sold comps and a line-item repair budget matter more than the percentage.
  • Always confirm with a full cost budget and a target-profit max price.
  • Stress-test ARV and rehab before you sign.
