BRRRR Calculator Walkthrough: How Much Cash Do You Really Get Back?

BRRRR stands for Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat. The pitch is simple: buy a fixer with short-term money, force appreciation with a rehab, rent it out, then refinance into a long-term loan and pull your cash back out to do it again.

The catch is in the word "back." How much comes back depends on a dozen numbers, and the ones people forget (points, holding costs, refinance closing costs) are exactly the ones that leave cash stuck in the deal. Let's walk one deal through, line by line.

Example numbers for illustration, not investment advice. Lenders' LTV limits, seasoning rules and appraisals vary.

The inputs

Buy
Purchase price $140,000
Purchase closing costs $4,000
Short-term loan (hard money / HELOC) $112,000
Short-term rate (interest-only) 11.00% per year
Points 2.0%
Rehab
Rehab budget $45,000
Months until refinance 6
Holding costs (taxes, insurance, utilities) $450 / month
Refinance
After-repair value (ARV) $240,000
Refinance LTV 75%
Refinance closing costs $5,000
New loan rate / term 6.90%, 30 years
Rent
Monthly rent $2,150
Vacancy / management / repairs + CapEx 5% / 8% / 10%
Taxes / insurance $2,400 / $1,400 per year

Step 1: Total project cost

  • Interest: $112,000 × 11% × 6/12 = $6,160
  • Points: $112,000 × 2% = $2,240
  • Financing cost: $8,400
  • Holding costs: $450 × 6 = $2,700

Total project cost = $140,000 + $4,000 + $45,000 + $8,400 + $2,700 = $200,100.

Step 2: Your cash in before the refinance

The short-term lender covered $112,000, so your own cash in is $200,100 − $112,000 = $88,100.

Step 3: The refinance

  • New loan: $240,000 × 75% = $180,000
  • Pay off the short-term loan: −$112,000
  • Refinance closing costs: −$5,000
  • Cash out to you: $63,000

Step 4: Cash left in the deal

$88,100 − $63,000 = $25,100 stays in the property.

That's not a failure. You now control a $240,000 property with $25,100 of your own money and $60,000 of equity ($240,000 − $180,000). But it's not the "infinite return" BRRRR is sometimes sold as, and you can't repeat the next deal with the same cash.

Step 5: Does it cash flow after the refinance?

  • New principal and interest on $180,000 at 6.9%: $1,185.48 / month
  • NOI: $2,150 − $107.50 vacancy − $163.40 management − $215.00 repairs/CapEx − $200.00 taxes − $116.67 insurance = $1,347.43
  • Cash flow: $161.95 / month
  • Cash-on-cash return: $161.95 × 12 ÷ $25,100 = 7.7%
  • DSCR: $1,347.43 ÷ $1,185.48 = 1.14

A DSCR of 1.14 means NOI covers the mortgage 1.14 times. Many DSCR lenders want 1.20 to 1.25, so this deal might need a lower LTV or higher rent to refinance with that kind of lender.

Step 6: Work backwards to the max price

The most useful BRRRR number is the highest purchase price that still returns all your cash at refinance:

Max price = ARV × LTV − refinance closing − purchase closing − rehab − financing cost − holding costs
          = $180,000 − $5,000 − $4,000 − $45,000 − $8,400 − $2,700
          = $114,900

At $140,000 we're paying $25,100 over that line, which is exactly the cash left in the deal. If your goal is a full cash-out, $114,900 is your ceiling (and in practice the financing cost shrinks a little at a lower price, so the true number is slightly higher).

The levers that matter most

  1. ARV. Every $10,000 of appraised value at 75% LTV returns $7,500 more cash. A conservative ARV from real sold comps protects you.
  2. Rehab overruns. Every dollar over budget is a dollar left in the deal. Add a contingency.
  3. Time. Each extra month adds interest and holding costs, and lenders may require a seasoning period before a cash-out refinance.
  4. Points and closing costs. Small percentages on big loans add up: here, $11,240 of the deal went to financing and closing costs.
