How Much Should Landlords Save for Repairs and CapEx?

Every rental has a few expensive surprises scheduled. You just don't know the exact dates. The roof, the HVAC system and the water heater will all need replacing eventually, and the landlords who stay calm when it happens are the ones who've been setting money aside every month.

This guide covers the two common ways to size that reserve and why the itemized method is worth 20 minutes of your time.

General planning guidance, not financial advice. Replace example lifespans and costs with your inspector's and contractors' numbers.

Repairs vs. CapEx

  • Repairs and maintenance: the small, frequent stuff. A leaky faucet, a broken garbage disposal, a service call.
  • Capital expenditures (CapEx): big replacements and improvements that happen every 10 to 30 years. Roof, HVAC, water heater, flooring, appliances, exterior paint, windows.

Keeping them separate also helps at tax time: repairs are usually expensed, while capital improvements are usually depreciated. Your tax preparer will want them split.

Method 1: Percent of rent

The common rule of thumb is to set aside 5–10% of rent for repairs and another 5–10% for CapEx. On $1,800 a month in rent, 5% to 10% for CapEx is $90 to $180 a month.

It's quick, and it's fine for screening a deal. But it ignores the actual condition of your property. A house with a brand-new roof and a 15-year-old one get the same reserve.

Method 2: Itemized reserve (the better way)

For each big-ticket component, estimate:

  1. Replacement cost (a quote or a reasonable local estimate)
  2. Expected life in years
  3. Current age

Then:

Years left      = expected life − age   (minimum 1)
Monthly reserve = replacement cost ÷ years left ÷ 12

Worked example: a single-family rental

Component Cost Life (yrs) Age (yrs) Years left Monthly reserve
Roof $12,000 25 15 10 $100.00
HVAC system $7,000 15 9 6 $97.22
Water heater $1,500 12 8 4 $31.25
Flooring $4,000 10 4 6 $55.56
Appliances $3,000 12 6 6 $41.67
Exterior paint $5,000 10 3 7 $59.52
Total $385.22

That's more than double the top of the 5–10% range ($180 a month) for this house. Not because the rule is wrong in general, but because this particular property has a mid-life roof and an aging HVAC system. If you'd been using the rule of thumb to judge cash flow, you'd be overestimating it by about $200 a month.

Over time, as items get replaced, their "years left" resets and the reserve falls. A property right after a full renovation might need well under 5%.

Where to keep the reserve

  • A separate savings account per property (or one account with a tracked balance per property).
  • Transfer the monthly reserve automatically on rent day.
  • Track the balance and what it's earmarked for, so the roof money doesn't get spent on a vacation.

Pair it with a maintenance log

A reserve is half the system. The other half is a work-order log: date, property, issue, category, priority, vendor, cost, status and days open. It shows you:

  • which properties eat the most repair money,
  • how fast jobs get closed (tenants notice),
  • which items are failing more often than expected (a sign to replace instead of repair),
  • and which costs were repairs vs. capital improvements for your tax preparer.

Key takeaways

  • Separate small repairs from big-ticket CapEx.
  • Percent-of-rent rules are fine for screening; an itemized reserve fits your actual property.
  • Monthly reserve = replacement cost ÷ years left ÷ 12.
  • Automate the transfer and keep a maintenance log alongside it.
