The gross rent multiplier formula

Gross rent multiplier (GRM) = purchase price ÷ gross annual rent. A $250,000 property that brings in $25,200 a year (that's $2,000 of rent plus $100 of other income, times 12) has a GRM of 9.92: it costs a little under ten years of rent. Investors flip the same formula around to value a property: value = gross annual rent × the GRM of comparable properties. If similar rentals in the area sell at about 10 times gross rent, this one is worth about $252,000.

The lower the GRM, the less you pay for each dollar of rent. It's popular for small multifamily and for quickly sorting listings because it needs only two numbers that are easy to get: the price and the rent roll.

Worked example

Gross income: ($2,000 rent + $100 other) × 12$25,200
GRM: $250,000 ÷ $25,2009.92
Value at a market GRM of 10: $25,200 × 10$252,000
Income needed at $250,000 for a GRM of 10: $250,000 ÷ (12 × 10)$2,083/mo
Monthly income ÷ price: $2,100 ÷ $250,0000.84%
Rough NOI at a 45% expense ratio: $25,200 × 0.55$13,860
Rough cap rate: 0.55 ÷ 9.925.54%

Compare it to a $300,000 property with $2,400 a month of rent ($28,800 a year). Its GRM is 10.42, so you'd pay more per dollar of rent, and at the same 45% expense ratio its rough cap rate is 5.28%. The first property looks better on both measures, though only the actual expenses can confirm it.

GRM, the 1% rule and the cap rate

The GRM and the 1% rule are the same comparison turned around. Rent of 1% of the price a month is a GRM of 8.33 (100 ÷ 12 ÷ 1); 0.8% is about 10.42; 2% is about 4.17. The cap rate adds what the GRM leaves out: it uses net income, so cap rate ≈ (1 − expense ratio) ÷ GRM. That's the shortcut behind the rough cap rate above. Two properties with the same GRM can have very different cap rates if one has high taxes, an old roof or an owner paying the utilities, so use the GRM to screen and the cap rate calculator and the rental property expense calculator before you offer.

What is a good gross rent multiplier?

There's no universal number. A lower GRM generally means more rent per dollar of price, but cheap-rent markets with low GRMs often have higher vacancy, older buildings and bigger repair bills, while expensive coastal markets run much higher multiples because buyers also count on appreciation. The useful benchmark is the GRM of comparable properties in the same area and class, which you can get from recent sales and their rent. Enter it as the target above to see whether a listing is priced above or below the market.

Limits of the GRM

  • It ignores vacancy and every operating expense, so it can't tell a lean property from an expensive one.
  • It ignores financing, so it says nothing about cash flow. Run your loan through the cash-on-cash return calculator and the DSCR calculator.
  • It depends on the rent being real: use actual leases or well-supported market rents, not the seller's wish.

FAQ

What is the gross rent multiplier?

It's the purchase price divided by the gross annual rent. A $250,000 property bringing in $25,200 a year has a GRM of 9.92. It shows how many years of gross rent it takes to equal the price, before vacancy, expenses or financing.

How do you calculate gross rent multiplier?

Add up the scheduled rent and other income for a year and divide the price by it. $250,000 ÷ ($2,100 × 12) = 9.92. To value a property, multiply its gross annual rent by the GRM of comparable properties.

What is a good GRM?

There isn't one number. Lower is cheaper per dollar of rent, but the right comparison is the GRM of similar properties in the same market, since taxes, rents and risk differ a lot. Compare a listing to recent comparable sales rather than to a national rule of thumb.

What is the difference between GRM and cap rate?

GRM uses gross rent, so it ignores expenses; the cap rate uses net operating income after vacancy and operating costs. As a shortcut, cap rate is roughly (1 - expense ratio) ÷ GRM, but the actual expenses of the property should replace that guess.

How does GRM relate to the 1% rule?

They're the same ratio inverted. Monthly rent of 1% of the price is a GRM of 8.33; 2% is 4.17. A GRM at or below 8.33 passes the 1% rule when the rent is the same figure.

Should I include vacancy in the GRM?

The standard GRM uses scheduled gross income, with no vacancy or expense deduction, which keeps comparisons simple. Account for vacancy and costs in the next step, with the cap rate or a full cash flow analysis.
