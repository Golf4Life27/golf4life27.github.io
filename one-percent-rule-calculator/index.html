The 1% rule formula

1% rule ratio = monthly rent ÷ all-in cost, where all-in cost is the purchase price plus any repairs needed before it can be rented. If the result is 1% or more, the property passes. A $200,000 house should rent for at least $2,000 a month; turn it around and the most you'd pay for a house that rents for $1,800 is $180,000, minus any rehab.

The 2% rule is the same test with a higher bar (a $100,000 house renting for $2,000). It's mostly reachable in low-price areas, which often come with higher vacancy, older buildings and more repairs. The gross rent multiplier is the flip side: all-in cost ÷ yearly rent. Passing the 1% rule is the same as a GRM of 8.33 or lower.

Worked example: a $200,000 house that needs $10,000 of work

All-in cost: $200,000 + $10,000 rehab$210,000
Rent-to-price ratio: $1,800 ÷ $210,0000.86% (below 1%)
Rent needed to pass: 1% × $210,000$2,100
Max price to pass: $1,800 ÷ 1% − $10,000$170,000
Mortgage: $150,000 (25% down) at 7% for 30 years$997.95/mo
Cash flow: $1,800 − 30% operating costs − $200 taxes − $100 insurance − $997.95−$37.95/mo
Break-even rent: ($997.95 + $200 + $100) ÷ 0.70$1,854 (0.88% of all-in)

The house fails the 1% rule, and the cash flow check agrees: it runs about $38 a month short. Here the ratio this loan needs to break even is 0.88%, so the rent would have to rise about $54 a month, or the price drop, before it pays for itself. Push the rent to $2,200 and it passes at 1.05% with about $242 a month of cash flow.

Does the 1% rule still work?

As a 10-second screen, yes. As a buying decision, no. The rule was popular when mortgages were 3% to 4%; at today's rates the same rent covers a much smaller loan, which is why some investors now screen at 1.2%. It also knows nothing about property taxes (which vary several-fold between states), insurance, HOA dues, how old the roof is, or your down payment. A property can pass 1% and still lose money: in the example, the same $2,200 rent with 50% operating costs (an older house with a manager) loses about $198 a month. That's why the calculator shows the ratio your loan and costs need, not just 1%.

FAQ

What is the 1% rule in real estate?

A quick screening rule: a rental's monthly rent should be at least 1% of its purchase price plus repairs. A $210,000 all-in house should rent for $2,100 a month or more. It's a filter for which listings deserve a full analysis, not proof a deal is good.

How do you calculate the 1% rule?

Divide the monthly rent by the purchase price plus rehab. $1,800 ÷ $210,000 = 0.86%, which is below 1%. To find the rent needed, multiply the all-in cost by 1%; to find the max price, divide the rent by 1% and subtract the rehab.

What is the 2% rule?

The same test with a 2% bar: monthly rent of at least 2% of the all-in cost, like $2,000 a month on a $100,000 house. It's rare outside low-price markets, and those properties often carry higher vacancy, repair and management costs.

Is the 1% rule realistic today?

In many high-cost and coastal markets few properties pass, and with mortgage rates near 7% passing 1% doesn't guarantee positive cash flow. Some investors screen at 1.2% instead. The best number is the break-even ratio for your own loan, taxes and costs, which this calculator shows.

Does the 1% rule include repairs and closing costs?

Most investors use the purchase price plus repairs needed before renting, so the rule reflects what you actually put into the property. Including closing costs makes it a little stricter.

What's the difference between the 1% rule and the gross rent multiplier?

They're the same comparison turned around. GRM = price ÷ yearly rent. Passing the 1% rule means a GRM of 8.33 or lower ($210,000 ÷ $25,200). The 2% rule means a GRM of about 4.17.
