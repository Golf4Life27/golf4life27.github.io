Printable Rental Property Record Book: What Belongs in a Landlord Binder

By KeyLedger Templates · Published 2026-10-08 · Updated 2026-10-30

Not every landlord wants another spreadsheet. If you own one to eight units and already keep a file drawer (or a binder in the glove box), a printable rental property record book can be the whole system: rent logs, expenses, deposits, maintenance, mileage and a year-end summary you can hand to a tax preparer.

This guide covers what to put in that binder, how the pages map to Schedule E categories, and when paper still beats software.

General record-keeping guidance for small landlords, not tax advice. Rules change and every situation is different, so confirm details with a qualified tax professional.

Why some landlords still prefer a printable binder

Spreadsheets and apps win when you have many units, partners or a property manager. Paper wins when:

  • You fill forms on a tablet or at the kitchen table and don't want another login
  • You want a physical audit trail for a local tax preparer
  • You manage a handful of units and ten minutes a month is enough
  • You already print lease packets and want one place for everything else

A good printable rental property record book is not a diary. It is a structured set of pages that mirror how landlords actually report income and expenses.

The pages that matter

1. Property and tenant snapshot

One page per unit: address, unit number, tenant name, lease start/end, rent amount, due day, deposit held, and emergency contact. Update it when a lease renews. This is the front of the binder — everything else hangs off it.

2. Rent payment log (up to 8 units)

A month-by-month grid: paid / late / partial / unpaid, with a notes column for partials and NSF. Eight units is the sweet spot for a printable binder; past that, a rent roll spreadsheet is usually faster.

3. Income and expense ledger (the bulk of the book)

Forty pages of dated lines with category dropdowns that map to Schedule E (Form 1040) Part I lines 3–19: rents received, advertising, auto and travel, cleaning and maintenance, commissions, insurance, legal, management fees, mortgage interest, repairs, supplies, taxes, utilities and other. Sorting as you go is what turns April into an hour instead of a weekend. For a digital version of the same idea, see how to track rental income and expenses for Schedule E.

4. Deposit register

Amount received, date, where it is held, return deadline and an itemized deduction list when a tenant moves out. State rules on deadlines and interest vary — leave a blank for your local deadline.

5. Maintenance and CapEx log

Date, unit, vendor, cost, and whether the work was a repair (expense) or a capital improvement (usually depreciated). That distinction matters at tax time.

6. Mileage log

Date, purpose, start/end odometer or miles, and a running total. Auto and travel is Schedule E line 6 for many landlords who drive to their properties.

7. Schedule E year-end summary

One page that rolls rent, each expense category and mileage into the lines your preparer expects. This is the page that makes a printable binder worth owning.

How to use the binder for 30 minutes a month

  1. Week of the 1st: mark rent paid or unpaid on the rent log.
  2. As expenses happen: write them on the next blank ledger line with the Schedule E category.
  3. After a service call: add a maintenance line; flag CapEx if it is a replacement or improvement.
  4. After any property trip: add mileage.
  5. January: copy the year-end summary and send it with your other docs. Start a fresh binder (or fresh ledger section) for the new year.

Printable binder vs. spreadsheet

Printable record book Spreadsheet tracker
Best for 1–8 units, paper-first landlords 5+ units, partners, remote bookkeeping
Tax handoff Print the summary page Export or share the Schedule E sheet
Search / totals Manual Instant filters and dashboards
Offline Always Needs the file open
Cost mindset One-time PDF, print what you need One-time template, reuse forever

Many landlords keep both: the binder in the car for move-ins and vendor visits, and a rental income and expense spreadsheet for year-end totals.
