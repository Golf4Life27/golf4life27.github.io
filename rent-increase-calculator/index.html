How to calculate a rent increase

There are two directions, and both use the current rent as the starting point:

  • Percent to new rent: new rent = current rent × (1 + increase ÷ 100). A 5% increase on $1,500 is $1,500 × 1.05 = $1,575.
  • New rent to percent: increase % = (new rent − current rent) ÷ current rent × 100. Going from $1,800 to $1,950 is $150 ÷ $1,800 = 8.3%.

Always divide by the old rent, not the new one. Dividing $150 by $1,950 gives 7.7%, which understates the increase and is a common mistake in renewal letters.

Worked example: a 5% renewal

Rent is $1,500 a month and the renewal offer is a 5% increase, repeated each year for five years.

Increase: $75 a month × 12$900.00 a year

Total rent over the 5 years$104,434.43
Extra vs. staying at $1,500 (5 × $18,000 = $90,000)$14,434.43

Each year's increase is figured on the previous year's rent, so the increases compound. That's why small yearly bumps add up: $2,000 rent rising 3% a year reaches about $4,713 a month by year 30, and the 30 years together total about $1.14 million.

For landlords: pricing a renewal

Look at the increase next to what a vacancy costs. One empty month on a $1,500 unit is $1,500 of lost rent, before cleaning, listing and turnover repairs, which is more than a full year of a 5% increase ($900). Many landlords keep renewals modest for good tenants and save market-rate jumps for turnover. Whatever you charge, put the new amount and the effective date in writing, record it on the tenant's ledger, and update your rent roll so next year's math starts from the right number.

Notice and local limits

How much notice a landlord must give, and whether there's a cap on the increase, depends on the lease and on state and local law, and the rules differ a lot from place to place. A fixed-term lease generally can't be raised mid-term unless the lease allows it. Several states and cities limit yearly increases or require longer notice for larger ones, so check the rules where the property is before sending a notice. This calculator does the math only; it doesn't know your local rules.

FAQ

How do I calculate a rent increase percentage?

Subtract the current rent from the new rent, divide by the current rent, and multiply by 100. Going from $1,800 to $1,950 is $150 ÷ $1,800 × 100 = 8.3%.

How do I find the new rent from a percent increase?

Multiply the current rent by 1 plus the increase as a decimal. A 5% increase on $1,500 is $1,500 × 1.05 = $1,575 a month.

What is a normal yearly rent increase?

It varies with the local market and the lease. Many landlords use modest yearly increases for renewing tenants and bigger adjustments between tenants. The best check is current asking rents for similar units nearby, plus any local cap that applies.

How much can a landlord raise the rent?

That depends on state and local law and the lease. Some places have no cap; several states and cities limit yearly increases or require longer notice for larger ones. Check the rules where the property is.

How much notice is needed for a rent increase?

It's set by the lease and local law, often at least 30 days for a month-to-month tenancy and longer in some places, especially for larger increases. A fixed-term lease usually can't be raised until it renews unless the lease says otherwise.

How do rent increases add up over several years?

They compound, because each year's increase is figured on the previous year's rent. $1,500 rent rising 5% a year is $1,914.42 by year 5, and over those 5 years the tenant pays $14,434.43 more than if rent had stayed at $1,500.
