How to Calculate Rental Property Cash Flow (With a Worked Example)

"Rent minus mortgage" is the most expensive math mistake in real estate investing. A house that rents for $1,950 with a $1,100 mortgage looks like it makes $850 a month. Run the full numbers and it might make $45.

Here's how to calculate cash flow properly, with one example carried all the way through.

Planning math only, not investment advice. Use real quotes for taxes, insurance and repairs whenever you can.

The formula

Gross income
− Vacancy loss
− Operating expenses (management, repairs, CapEx, taxes, insurance, HOA, utilities)
= Net operating income (NOI)
− Mortgage payment (principal + interest)
= Cash flow

The example property

Input Value
Purchase price $220,000
Down payment 25% ($55,000)
Closing costs + upfront repairs $8,000
Interest rate / term 7.00%, 30 years
Rent $1,950 / month
Vacancy 5% of rent
Property management 8% of collected rent
Repairs + CapEx reserve 10% of rent
Property taxes $3,000 / year
Insurance $1,400 / year

Step 1: Gross income and vacancy

Gross income is $1,950. Even good properties sit empty between tenants, so subtract a vacancy allowance: 5% × $1,950 = $97.50. That leaves $1,852.50 of expected collected rent.

Step 2: Operating expenses

Expense Math Monthly
Management 8% × $1,852.50 $148.20
Repairs + CapEx reserve 10% × $1,950 $195.00
Property taxes $3,000 ÷ 12 $250.00
Insurance $1,400 ÷ 12 $116.67
Total $709.87

"But I'll self-manage," you might say. Budget for management anyway. Your time has value, and if you ever hand the property off, the deal still has to work.

Repairs and CapEx (big-ticket replacements like roofs, HVAC and water heaters) are the expenses new investors most often skip, because they don't show up every month. They show up as a $9,000 bill in year four.

Step 3: Net operating income

$1,950 − $97.50 − $709.87 = $1,142.63 per month ($13,711.60 per year).

NOI ignores financing. That's why it's used for cap rate.

Step 4: The mortgage

Loan amount: $220,000 × 75% = $165,000. At 7% for 30 years, principal and interest come to about $1,097.75 per month. In a spreadsheet:

=PMT(7%/12, 30*12, -165000)

Step 5: Cash flow

$1,142.63 − $1,097.75 = $44.88 per month, or about $539 a year.

Positive, but thin. One surprise repair beyond the reserve, or a two-month vacancy, and the year goes negative.

The three ratios worth knowing

Cap rate = annual NOI ÷ price = $13,711.60 ÷ $220,000 = 6.23%. It compares properties regardless of how they're financed.

Cash-on-cash return = annual cash flow ÷ cash invested. Cash invested is the $55,000 down payment plus $8,000 closing and repairs, so $63,000. $538.61 ÷ $63,000 = 0.85%. That's what your actual cash earns this year, before appreciation and loan paydown.

The 1% rule = monthly rent ÷ price = $1,950 ÷ $220,000 = 0.89%. It's a quick screen: properties under 1% often struggle to cash flow at today's rates. It's a filter, not a verdict.

What would make this deal work?

Change one input at a time and watch cash flow:

  • Negotiate the price to $200,000: the loan drops to $150,000, the payment to about $998, and cash flow rises to roughly $145 a month.
  • Find $150 more rent (say, by adding a washer and dryer): cash flow rises by about $116, because vacancy, management and reserves scale with rent.
  • Put 30% down: cash flow rises to about $118 and cash-on-cash to about 1.9%, but you tie up $11,000 more cash.

This is why a calculator beats napkin math. You can test five versions of a deal in five minutes.

Key takeaways

  • Cash flow = NOI − mortgage, and NOI already includes vacancy, management and reserves.
  • Budget for management and CapEx even if you self-manage and the roof is new.
  • Cap rate compares properties; cash-on-cash measures your cash; the 1% rule is a fast filter.
  • Run several what-if versions before you make an offer.
