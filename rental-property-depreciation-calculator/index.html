How rental property depreciation works

The IRS treats a residential rental building as wearing out over 27.5 years, so each year you deduct a slice of what it cost, even though no cash leaves your pocket. The usual method for residential rentals is straight line with the mid-month convention. Here's the math the calculator does:

  1. Find the cost basis: purchase price plus closing costs you capitalize (title, recording, transfer taxes and similar; not loan points or prepaid interest, which are handled separately).
  2. Take out the land. Land never depreciates. Split the cost between land and building, often using the property tax assessment's land and improvement values as the ratio.
  3. Add improvements made before the property was first ready to rent. Improvements added later get their own 27.5-year schedule starting when they're placed in service.
  4. Divide by 27.5 for the full-year amount. In the first year, the mid-month rule treats the property as placed in service in the middle of its month, so a June start gets 6.5 of 12 months.

Depreciation starts when the property is placed in service, meaning ready and available to rent, not the closing date or the day the first tenant moves in. It ends when the basis is used up (the 28th or 29th tax year) or when you sell or stop renting it.

Worked example: a $300,000 rental placed in service in June

Purchase price plus $6,000 capitalized closing costs$306,000.00
Land at 20% of cost (not depreciable)$61,200.00
Depreciable building basis$244,800.00
Full-year depreciation: $244,800 / 27.5$8,901.82
First year, June start: $244,800 x 6.5 / 12 / 27.5$4,821.82
Total after 10 tax years: $4,821.82 + 9 x $8,901.82$84,938.18

At a 24% tax rate, each full year of depreciation is worth about $2,136 in tax, if your rental losses are deductible that year (passive loss limits can delay the benefit; the suspended loss carries forward). When you sell after 10 years, the $84,938 taken is generally taxed as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at your ordinary rate, capped at 25%: about $20,385 here. A January start would give a first-year rate of 3.485% instead of 1.97%.

What about bonus depreciation and cost segregation?

The building itself is 27.5-year property and doesn't qualify for bonus depreciation. A cost segregation study can split out parts like appliances, carpet, cabinets and land improvements (fencing, driveways, landscaping) into 5-, 7- and 15-year classes, which can qualify for bonus depreciation and front-load the deduction. That's worth a conversation with a tax pro on larger properties. This calculator covers the standard 27.5-year building schedule only, and it's an estimate, not tax advice.

Keep the records that support it

Depreciation goes on Form 4562 and flows to Schedule E, and the IRS reduces your basis by depreciation allowed or allowable, so you get recaptured at sale even if you forgot to claim it. Keep the closing statement, the land and building split you used, the placed-in-service date and a running depreciation schedule with your rental records. FAQ

How do you calculate depreciation on a rental property?

Start with the purchase price plus capitalized closing costs, subtract the land value, add improvements made before renting, and divide by 27.5 for the yearly deduction. For a $244,800 building basis that's $8,901.82 a year. The first year is prorated by the mid-month rule.

Why is rental property depreciated over 27.5 years?

That's the recovery period the IRS sets for residential rental property under MACRS, the general depreciation system. Commercial buildings use 39 years. Residential rental means at least 80% of the building's rent comes from dwelling units.

How does the mid-month convention work in the first year?

The property is treated as placed in service in the middle of its month. Placed in service in June, you get 6.5 months of the year: 6.5 / 12 / 27.5 = 1.97% of the basis. January gives 3.485% and December gives 0.152%. The leftover half month is picked up at the end of the schedule.

How do I figure out the land value?

A common method is the ratio from your property tax assessment: if the assessor values land at 20% of the total, use 20% of your cost as land. An appraisal or insurance replacement cost can also support the split. Keep whatever you used with your records.

What is depreciation recapture?

When you sell, the depreciation you took (or could have taken) is generally taxed as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at your ordinary rate, capped at 25%, before the rest of the gain gets capital gains rates. A 1031 exchange can defer it.

Can I skip depreciation to avoid recapture?

No. The IRS reduces your basis by the depreciation allowed or allowable, so recapture applies whether you claimed it or not. Skipping it loses the deduction and keeps the tax at sale. Missed years can usually be caught up with a change in accounting method; ask a tax pro.
