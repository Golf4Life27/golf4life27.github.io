What counts as a rental property expense?

Operating expenses are the recurring costs of keeping a rental running: property taxes, landlord insurance, HOA or service charges, utilities you cover, lawn and snow care, repairs and maintenance, a reserve for big replacements (roof, HVAC, appliances), and property management if you pay for it. Vacancy isn't a bill, but it's rent you don't collect, so it belongs in the same total. Mortgage principal and interest are not operating expenses: they're financing costs, and you subtract them later to get cash flow. Depreciation is a tax deduction, not cash out of your pocket, so it's left out here too.

What's left after operating expenses and vacancy is net operating income (NOI), the number behind the cap rate, the net rental yield and the lender's DSCR.

Total costs with vacancy: $9,202 + $1,080$10,282 a year ($857 a month)
Expense ratio: $10,282 ÷ $21,60047.6%
Net operating income: $20,520 − $9,202$11,318
Cap rate on a $250,000 price4.53%
Break-even rent: $5,400 fixed ÷ (12 × 0.774 left after the percent costs)$581/mo

Almost half of every rent dollar goes to the property before the mortgage, which is why a house that looks great against its mortgage can still be a weak buy. The break-even rent is the floor: at $581 a month the rent only pays the running costs, with nothing for the loan and nothing for you.

The 50% rule, and how to use it

The 50% rule is a rough screening shortcut: assume operating expenses, including vacancy, will eat about half of the rent, then compare the leftover to the mortgage payment. It's quick for a first look at a listing, but it's only a rule of thumb. Newer, lower-tax properties often run 30% to 40%, and old buildings with high taxes, HOA fees or an owner paying utilities can run well past 50%. Use it to sanity-check the real number this calculator builds from the actual line items, and see where yours lands against the 1% rule as well and the gross rent multiplier.

Typical rules of thumb for the percentage lines

  • Repairs and maintenance: often budgeted around 5% to 10% of rent, or roughly 1% of the property value a year for older homes.
  • Capex reserve: many investors set aside another 5% to 10% for roofs, HVAC, water heaters and flooring. The capex reserves guide shows how to size it from each item's age and cost.
  • Management: commonly 8% to 10% of collected rent for a long-term rental, plus a leasing fee on new tenants. Enter 0 if you self-manage, but keep in mind your time isn't free.
  • Vacancy: 5% to 8% is a common planning figure, which is about 2.5 to 4 weeks empty a year.

These are planning numbers, not promises. Your own tax bill, insurance quote and repair history beat any rule of thumb. FAQ

What expenses should I include for a rental property?

Property taxes, landlord insurance, HOA or service charges, utilities you pay, lawn and snow care, repairs and maintenance, a capex reserve for big replacements, property management and an allowance for vacancy. Leave out the mortgage payment and depreciation: the mortgage is financing, and depreciation is a tax deduction rather than a cash cost.

What is the 50% rule for rental properties?

It's a screening shortcut that assumes operating expenses, including vacancy, will use about 50% of the gross rent. You compare the other 50% to the mortgage payment to see if the deal might cash flow. It's only a rule of thumb, so run the real numbers before you buy.

How much are typical rental property expenses?

Most rentals land somewhere between 30% and 50% of scheduled rent for operating expenses and vacancy together, depending on property taxes, age, HOA fees and who pays utilities. In the example above, a $1,800-a-month rental has $10,282 of yearly costs, or 47.6% of rent.

Is the mortgage a rental property expense?

Not for operating-expense purposes. Operating expenses exclude loan principal and interest so that net operating income and cap rate can compare properties however they're financed. You subtract the mortgage after NOI to get cash flow, though mortgage interest is deductible on your tax return.

What is the break-even rent?

It's the monthly rent at which net operating income is zero: the rent pays the taxes, insurance, repairs, management and vacancy and nothing else. Because several costs are a percent of rent, the calculator solves for it directly. To cover a mortgage payment as well, the rent has to be higher.

How much should I budget for repairs and capex?

Many landlords plan about 5% to 10% of rent for everyday repairs and another 5% to 10% for big-ticket replacements, more for older properties. If you're not sure, build the reserve from the age and cost of the roof, HVAC, water heater and flooring instead of using a flat percentage.
