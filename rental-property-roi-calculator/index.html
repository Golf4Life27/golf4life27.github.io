How to calculate ROI on a rental property

A rental pays you four ways: cash flow (rent left after vacancy, running costs and the mortgage), loan paydown (each payment's principal is equity the tenant bought for you), appreciation and, in the US, tax savings from depreciation. This calculator counts the first three and takes off selling costs and the closing costs you paid going in:

Total profit = total cash flow + loan paydown + appreciation − selling costs − closing costs. Total ROI = total profit ÷ cash invested, where cash invested is your down payment plus closing costs and repairs. Annualized ROI spreads that over the years you hold it, and IRR also gives credit for cash that arrives early (yearly cash flow) versus late (the sale). Taxes and depreciation are left out; see the depreciation calculator for that piece.

Worked example: a $250,000 rental held 5 years

Cash invested: 25% down ($62,500) + $8,000 closing and repairs$70,500
Loan $187,500 at 7% for 30 years$1,247/mo
Year one: $24,000 rent − 5% vacancy − $6,000 costs − $14,969 mortgage$1,831 cash flow (2.60% cash-on-cash)
Cash flow over 5 years, rent and costs up 3% a year$14,347
Loan paid down: $187,500 − $176,497 left$11,003
Appreciation at 3% a year: $289,819 − $250,000$39,819
Less 6% selling costs and the $8,000 closing costs−$25,389
Total profit$39,780
Total ROI: $39,780 ÷ $70,50056.4% (9.36% a year annualized, 9.86% IRR)

A 2.6% cash-on-cash return looks weak on its own, yet the whole deal returns close to 10% a year once loan paydown and appreciation are counted. That's also the risk: most of the profit here depends on the 3% appreciation. Set appreciation to 0% and the same deal makes only about $2,350 over 5 years (under 1% a year), which is why many investors want cash flow to carry the deal and treat appreciation as a bonus. Hold it only 1 year and selling costs turn it into a loss of about $12,000.

What is a good ROI on a rental property?

Many investors aim for a total return of 8% to 12% a year or more, enough to beat a stock index fund for the extra work and risk of owning a rental. Compare the annualized ROI or IRR, not the total ROI, when two deals have different hold periods. FAQ

How do you calculate ROI on a rental property?

Add up the cash flow you collect, the loan principal paid down and the appreciation, then subtract selling costs and the closing costs you paid. Divide that profit by the cash you put in. In the example, $39,780 profit on $70,500 invested is a 56.4% total ROI over 5 years.

What's the difference between ROI and cash-on-cash return?

Cash-on-cash return is one year's cash flow divided by the cash you invested. ROI over a hold period also counts loan paydown, appreciation and the cost of selling, so it measures the whole investment rather than one year's income.

What is a good ROI on a rental property?

Many investors look for 8% to 12% a year or more in total return, measured as annualized ROI or IRR. A deal that only hits that number with strong appreciation is riskier than one where cash flow does most of the work.

What's the difference between annualized ROI and IRR?

Annualized ROI treats all the profit as if it arrived at the end. IRR gives credit for cash flow you collect along the way, so it's usually a little higher when the property cash flows. Both are yearly rates you can compare between deals with different hold periods.

Does this include taxes and depreciation?

No. Income tax on the rent, depreciation deductions, depreciation recapture and capital gains tax depend on your situation, so they're left out. Treat the result as a pre-tax return and check the tax side with a tax professional.

Why is my ROI negative in the first year?

Buying and selling costs are large. Paying closing costs going in and about 6% to sell means a short hold often loses money even when the property cash flows. Most rentals need several years for paydown and appreciation to cover those costs.
