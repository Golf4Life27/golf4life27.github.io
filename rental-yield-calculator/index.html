Rental yield formulas

Gross rental yield = yearly rent ÷ purchase price. It's the headline number on listings and the easiest to compare, but it ignores every cost of owning the place.

Net rental yield = (yearly rent − vacancy − running costs) ÷ total cost, where total cost is the price plus buying costs and any repairs before the first tenant. Running costs are property taxes, insurance, repairs, management and HOA or service charges. Mortgage payments are left out on purpose, so net yield compares properties no matter how you finance them. Measured on the price alone, net yield is the same thing as the cap rate.

Worked example: a $250,000 rental at $1,800 a month

Yearly rent: $1,800 × 12$21,600
Gross yield: $21,600 ÷ $250,0008.64%
Less 5% vacancy ($1,080) and $6,000 running costs$14,520 net a year
Total cost: $250,000 + $10,000 buying costs and repairs$260,000
Net yield: $14,520 ÷ $260,0005.58%
After 20% income tax: $11,616 ÷ $260,0004.47%
Rent for a 6% net yield: ($15,600 + $6,000) ÷ (12 × 0.95)$1,895/mo
Max price for 6% net: $14,520 ÷ 6% − $10,000$232,000

The same house that looks like an 8.64% yield on the listing keeps 5.58% once a few empty weeks and the yearly bills come out. About a third of the rent (32.8%) never reaches you. To earn 6% net, the rent would need to be about $95 a month higher, or the price about $18,000 lower.

What is a good rental yield?

There's no single answer, because yield trades off against growth and risk. Expensive, fast-appreciating cities often show gross yields of 4% to 6%, while cheaper markets can show 9% or more with higher vacancy, older buildings and more repairs. Many investors look for a net yield above what they could earn risk-free (savings or Treasury rates) plus a margin for the work and risk of being a landlord. Compare net yields, never a gross yield on one property against a net yield on another.

FAQ

How do you calculate rental yield?

Gross rental yield is yearly rent divided by the purchase price: $21,600 ÷ $250,000 = 8.64%. Net rental yield subtracts vacancy and running costs from the rent and divides by the total cost including buying costs: $14,520 ÷ $260,000 = 5.58%.

What's the difference between gross and net rental yield?

Gross yield uses the full rent and ignores costs. Net yield uses what's left after empty months, property taxes, insurance, repairs, management and service charges. Net is always lower, often by a third or more, and it's the one that tells you what the property really earns.

What is a good rental yield?

It depends on the market. High-cost, high-growth areas often show 4% to 6% gross, while cheaper areas can show 8% to 10% or more with more vacancy and repair risk. A common test is whether the net yield beats a risk-free savings rate by enough to pay you for the work and risk.

Does rental yield include the mortgage?

No. Yield is measured before financing so you can compare properties fairly. To see the effect of your loan, use cash-on-cash return or a cash flow calculation that includes the mortgage payment.

Is net rental yield the same as cap rate?

Nearly. Cap rate is net operating income divided by the price or value. Net yield on the price alone is the same number; this calculator's main net yield also adds buying costs and repairs to the price, so it's slightly lower.

How is the after-tax yield worked out?

It simply takes your tax rate off the net income. Real rental tax depends on where you live, mortgage interest rules and depreciation, so treat it as a rough guide and check with a tax professional.
