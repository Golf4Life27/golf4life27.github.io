How to Track Rental Income and Expenses for Schedule E

By KeyLedger Templates · Published 2026-10-08 · Updated 2026-10-19

If you own one to ten rentals and do your own books, tax season usually goes one of two ways: you open a tidy summary and send it to your preparer, or you spend a weekend digging through bank statements, email receipts and a drawer of paper. The difference isn't accounting skill. It's whether you set up a simple system in January and spent ten minutes a month on it.

This guide walks through that system. It works with any tool, from a notebook to accounting software. We'll use a spreadsheet because it's free, flexible and easy to hand to a tax preparer.

This is general record-keeping guidance, not tax advice. Rules change and every situation is different, so confirm the details with a qualified tax professional.

Step 1: Use the Schedule E categories from day one

US landlords report rental income and expenses on Schedule E (Form 1040), Part I. If you sort every transaction into those lines as you go, your year-end summary basically builds itself. The expense lines are:

Line Category Common examples
3 Rents received Monthly rent, late fees, pet rent
5 Advertising Listing fees, signs
6 Auto and travel Mileage to the property, trips to the hardware store
7 Cleaning and maintenance Turnover cleaning, lawn care, pest control
8 Commissions Leasing commissions
9 Insurance Landlord / dwelling policy
10 Legal and other professional fees Attorney, tax prep, bookkeeping
11 Management fees Property manager's monthly percentage
12 Mortgage interest paid to banks The interest portion of your payment, not principal
13 Other interest Interest on other loans used for the rental
14 Repairs Fixing a leak, patching drywall, replacing a broken part
15 Supplies Light bulbs, filters, smoke detector batteries
16 Taxes Property taxes
17 Utilities Owner-paid water, trash, electric
18 Depreciation expense or depletion Usually calculated by your preparer
19 Other Anything that doesn't fit above (HOA dues, software, bank fees)

Two mistakes show up again and again:

  1. Logging the whole mortgage payment as an expense. Only the interest goes on line 12. Principal isn't deductible, and escrowed taxes and insurance belong on their own lines.
  2. Expensing improvements as repairs. A new roof, a full kitchen remodel or a new HVAC system is usually a capital improvement that gets depreciated over time, not a repair. Keep improvements on a separate list so your preparer can decide how to treat them.

Step 2: One log, one row per transaction

Make a single transaction log with these columns:

  • Date
  • Property (pick from a list so spelling never varies)
  • Type (income or expense)
  • Category (pick from the Schedule E list above)
  • Payee or payer
  • Description
  • Amount
  • Payment method
  • Receipt saved? (yes/no)

Dropdowns matter more than you'd think. If one month you type "Maple St" and the next "Maple Street," your per-property totals break. A list of properties and a list of categories on a settings tab fixes that for good.

Step 3: Track mileage as you go

Driving to show a unit, meet a contractor or pick up supplies can add up to a meaningful deduction, but only if you have a log. Record the date, property, purpose and miles for each trip. At year-end, multiply total business miles by the IRS standard mileage rate for that year (check IRS.gov for the current rate) or give the log to your preparer.

Step 4: Keep capital improvements on their own tab

Give improvements their own short list: date, property, description, cost. It keeps them out of your repairs total and gives your preparer exactly what they need for depreciation.

Step 5: The 10-minute monthly routine

Pick a day, like the 5th of each month after rent comes in, and do this:

  1. Download or open last month's bank and card activity for your rental account.
  2. Add each rental transaction to the log. With dropdowns this takes a few minutes.
  3. Snap or save any receipts you haven't saved yet and mark "Receipt saved?"
  4. Add any trips to the mileage log.
  5. Glance at the monthly summary: did anything look off, like a utility bill that doubled?

Doing this monthly beats doing it once a year for a simple reason: you still remember what last month's $86 hardware-store charge was for.

Tip: Use a separate bank account (and ideally a separate card) for your rentals. It's the single biggest time-saver in landlord bookkeeping.

Step 6: Build the year-end summary

If every transaction has a property and a Schedule E category, the summary is just a table of totals: one row per Schedule E line and one column per property. In a spreadsheet that's a SUMIFS per cell:

=SUMIFS(Amount, Property, "Maple Duplex", Category, "Repairs")

Add a dashboard with income, expenses and net profit by month, and you can also spot problems mid-year instead of in April.
